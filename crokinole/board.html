The Picket Board

Crokinole taken off the circle and laid on a rectangle. There is no hole: the centre is a raised Crown that rejects a disc arriving too slow and lets a disc arriving too fast ride across and off the far side. A picket of pegs fences it; which edges cushion and which are open ditch is a property of the board, not of the game. Four boards ship — a lane, a courtyard, a gauntlet and a terrace — and every number in all four lives in one authored file, layouts.json, so a board is a data edit and a fifth can be pasted in below without a rebuild. So is the rack: twelve named discs a side, eight stats each, every stat bound to a term the solver actually evaluates. The opponent searches by running the same physics you are watching — and it plays the same twelve discs, which is why it can be told what a steady hand is worth without being lied to.

    Rules implemented — the numbers, and where this board bends them

    The board is data, and the data is a file

    There is no board constant anywhere in the renderer, the rules or the opponent's search. Every dimension comes out of one entry in a layouts table: the surface half-extents, the edge list (each segment a rail or a ditch), the ordered scoring zones — each one either a rectangle or an explicit convex polygon — the Crown's outline and its per-face climb thresholds, the picket as explicit peg coordinates, and the launch segments. Switching boards in the panel above swaps one object; nothing else changes.

    That table is authored in layouts.json, not in this page. The page carries it in a <script type="application/json" id="board-layouts"> block which the build regenerates from the file, and ships the file alongside the page so the two can be diffed. It is inlined rather than fetched for one reason: this page has to open straight from file://, and a fetch of a sibling file is blocked there by the same-origin rule. So there is exactly one hand- maintained copy of the geometry — the JSON — and the block in this page is generated from it. The Load a board panel above reads the same schema at runtime, which is how a fifth board gets tried without touching the repository.

    The four boards, and what each one is for

    They are not four skins. Each changes a different thing about what a good shot is.

    • Lane · 48 × 24. A 2:1 rectangle shot down its long axis. The long sides bank; both short ends are open ditch. Two fences flank the Crown, and their posts are staggered against each other so that no straight line passes through both — the widest corridor through the pair is 0.0005″ against 1.375″ through either fence alone. Error along the lane is lethal and error across it is forgiven, so straight is the power route and the rail is the touch route.
    • Courtyard · 32 × 32. Square, four launch lines, and every edge is open ditch — there is no cushion anywhere and nothing forgives. Its zones are diamonds, which is why a polygon zone kind exists: a diamond made out of rectangles would be a lie told in the scorer as well as in the paint. Eight posts ring the Crown on the cardinals and the diagonals, so the shooter's straight-ahead line is shut and the ways in are off-axis. This is the deliberate inversion of real crokinole, which hands you the open twenty straight in front of you.
    • Gauntlet · 56 × 20. Narrow, and fully enclosed: every edge is a rail, so nothing is ever lost over the side. With no clearing the board only fills, and the game becomes congestion, caroms and burying. A chevron faces each shooter, convex toward them, so the straight lane at the Crown is 0.575″ wide — under half a disc — and every reliable route banks or threads a gate at an angle. Because nothing is ever removed, this board needs a rule about being full; see below.
    • Terrace · 36 × 28. Shot across the short axis at a Crown that is not a point but a stripe spanning the full width of the board, rail to rail. That changes what you are aiming at: there is no left or right to get wrong, only depth. Overshoot and you sail past the stripe into their approach, or off the far end. Two colonnades of posts, seven against six so the rows interleave, sit on the two approaches. Discs land in a row rather than a cluster, and holding the stripe is a line you can be pushed off.

    The discs are data too

    You do not hold twelve identical discs. You hold a named rack — the same rack the machine holds — and playing a disc is what it costs. There is no draft, no shuffle, no draw, no hidden information and no second resource: the disc is the spend gate. Both racks are face up beside the board at all times.

    Every disc is eight numbers, and every one of the eight binds to a term the solver actually evaluates — there are no decorative stats. mass is the reduced mass in the collision impulse and the split of positional de-overlap; radius is the contact distance against discs, posts and cushions, so it decides what can thread the picket; e is disc-disc restitution and the pair takes the minimum, so the deader disc governs; pegE and railE are the bounces off a post and off a cushion; fric multiplies the deceleration, so it is how far the disc runs per unit power; lipMul scales the Crown's climb threshold on the way in and the refund on the way out, so a round trip stays exactly energy-neutral at any value.

    DiscLineEffect, and what it costs

    The eighth stat, and the honest opponent

    The eighth stat is tremorMul, and it is the only one that does not live inside the physics step: it scales the Gaussian sigma on the released angle and power, and it never biases the mean. That makes it the one stat a noiseless search is blind to — run the old single-pass search and a Compass scores exactly like a Plumb, so the machine would hoard it or spend it at random while this page told you it was precise. That would be a lie, and it is the specific lie this page exists to avoid.

    So the search has two stages. Stage one is the old noiseless pass, widened: it now loops over every distinct disc still in the machine's rack and simulates each candidate with that disc's real stat block, resolved through the same release hook you get. Stage two re-runs the best sixteen with the hand applied, and the shape of that re-run is the part that decides whether the row means anything. Four hands are dealt once, from a search substream seeded from the match seed, the round and the shot number — a stream that never touches the game's own generator, so the machine looking at a shot cannot change your game — and then every candidate is shot with those same four hands. Common random numbers: a difference between two candidates' robust scores is a difference between the shots, not between the wobbles they happened to draw. (Independent draws per candidate is the trap here. At four samples the spread from draw luck alone is larger than any board term on the table, so the ranking measures luck and calls it robustness.) The four are antithetic — each wobble is paired with its exact negative — so the sample set has mean zero by construction rather than by hope. The candidates are ranked on a blend of the mean and the worst of the four. A Compass's samples cluster; a Plumb's spread. That gap is printed as its own row in the table above, and it is the entire reason the assist is allowed to exist.

    The assist is priced for both sides, and that is a setting you can break. The machine draws from the identical twelve. Its Compass is one of its twelve exactly as yours is one of yours, it is spent when it is played, and you can watch its rack empty in the panel. Both hands shake at the same sigma by default, so the disc buys each side the same thing. The one asymmetry available is the one you choose: switch your own hand off in the panel and your release becomes exact, at which point a steadying disc costs you its stat line and buys you nothing — there is no wobble left to remove. The rack panel flags that on the disc itself while the setting is off, rather than letting you pay for it quietly.

    Where an effect is allowed to live

    • onRelease — outside the simulation, after the placement, angle and power are fixed. It may rewrite the shooter's stat block. It may not read a board position, the generator or the clock, and it cannot trigger another hook. Echo's copy runs here, off an append-only list of the disc types you have already shot this round, which is why a replay reproduces it by construction.
    • contactOverride — inside the physics step, at the moment a contact resolves, before the impulse. It returns a restitution and nothing else: no extra impulse, no teleport, no removal, no mass change. "Which disc is incoming" is defined as the one with the greater speed along the contact normal, ties broken by the lower id — never as whichever the loop happened to reach first, because that would make the same board resolve differently depending on the order the discs were created.
    • fieldDecel — inside the friction pass, every step, for every moving disc. It returns added deceleration and it is summed into the friction magnitude, which is not a stylistic choice: because it is added to friction it inherits the existing stop threshold and is mathematically incapable of creating motion, reversing a velocity, or preventing a disc from coming to rest. An attractor could do all three. This cannot.
    • tremorScale — the hand, and only the hand. The noise function always draws exactly two Gaussian values and then scales them, so the number of draws is invariant to the sigma and to the multiplier. (The version of this file that returned early at zero sigma consumed no draws, which would have silently desynchronised the whole stream the first time a steady disc was played.)

    Rules enforced

    • The Crown. The centre is a raised convex plateau — a rectangle on three of the four boards, a diamond on the Courtyard — with a chamfered lip. Each face carries its own climb threshold. Crossing the lip inwards costs a fixed amount of squared normal speed: below the threshold the disc is turned away and rebounds off the chamfer; above it, the disc climbs and keeps the remainder. Crossing outwards refunds the same amount — a disc on the Crown is standing on a downhill, so knocking one off is easier than putting one on. On the deck, friction is multiplied, so a disc that crests slowly stops quickly and a disc that crests fast rides across and off the far side.
    • There is no hole. Nothing sinks. The centre scores by occupancy.
    • Placement. Your disc is set anywhere along your own launch segment — a straight line inset from your ditch end. Sliding along it changes your distance, your angle and your rail access at once, so where you stand is a decision separate from where you aim.
    • The Engage rule. Two legal answers. If the opponent has any disc in a live zone, your shot is legal if it either contacts one (directly or through a carom) or comes to rest on the Crown. If they have no live disc anywhere, only the Crown answer is legal. Failing both is a foul: your shooting disc is removed, and so is every disc of yours that moved.
    • Rails and ditch. A rail segment bounces — the normal component is scaled by restitution, the tangential component is scraped by a drag factor — and can never remove a disc. A ditch segment removes: a centre past it is out of the round. Which edges are which is layout data, and the loader refuses — not warns — a layout that does not leave at least one ditch segment behind each player, or that names a gate which is not an opening, or whose zones are not convex. A refused board does not load: the one in play stays in play, the picker snaps back to it, and the refusal is written to the shot log.
    • The board-full rule. A board may declare itself enclosed, and then it must be enclosed on every segment — the audit checks the claim rather than taking it. The Gauntlet does. Nothing is ever removed there by falling off, so the board can only fill, and something has to say what happens when it is full. The round ends the moment the side to move has no legal placement anywhere along its launch line, and the board scores exactly as it stands, unspent discs and all. The alternative — let the stuck player pass — was rejected because it hands the last word to whoever is holding fewer discs, which on a board that only fills rewards having already spent badly. Ending it makes a jam a position: you close the board while you are ahead on it, and your opponent's remaining discs are worth nothing. The check sweeps the whole segment at the resolution of the placement slider's finest step, using the smallest body still in your hand, so "there is nowhere to stand" and "I could not find anywhere to stand" cannot disagree. The rule is enforced on every board; on the other three the ditch keeps it from ever firing. Fouling still removes discs everywhere, including here — that is a rule about the shot, not about the edge, and it is the Gauntlet's only way to take anything off.
    • Scoring a round. Zones are convex regions — rectangles or explicit polygons — tested most-specific-first, first containment wins. Crown 3, mid 2, outer 1, apron 0: the outermost band is dead, so parking there is worth nothing and makes the disc untargetable under the Engage rule. Holding the Crown while the opponent holds none adds a sweep bonus. The higher side banks the difference only.
    • The loser's hammer. Whoever banked points last round shoots first this round; a tied round leaves the order alone. Under occupancy scoring the last shot of a round is worth a great deal, so scoring big partly taxes itself.

    Where this board approximates

    Zone value is decided by the disc's centre. A real line rule would demand the disc be wholly inside. Centre-based scoring is a uniform simplification applied consistently — to the Crown, to the aprons and to the dead bands alike.

    The lip is a toll, not a ramp. The Crown's rise is resolved as one impulse at the boundary rather than as a slope integrated across the chamfer. A disc turned away is clamped back outside the lip, which can leave it overlapping by at most one substep of travel — 0.117″ at the maximum shot speed — and that resolves on the following substep. A disc that crosses an x-boundary and a y-boundary in the same substep (a corner entry) pays both tolls.

    Resting on the lip is snapped outward. A disc that crests with almost nothing left would otherwise score 3 or 1 on a sixth-decimal float. Any disc at rest within the snap distance of a Crown edge is pushed outside it — ties always resolve against the Crown, so the ledge is visible rather than infinitesimal.

    No spin, no bevel, no thickness. Discs are flat circles. They differ in mass and in radius, but a "concave running band" or a "dead edge" is expressed as a number, not as geometry. The Crown's rise is a scalar toll, not a height field; nothing rides up on anything.

    Echo copies stats, not effects — including the hand. tremorMul is one of the eight, so an Echo racked straight after a Compass is as steady as the Compass was. That is the sequencing tax working, not leaking: you spent the Compass first, in public, and the copy still pays the wear tax on friction.

    Foul bookkeeping. "Discs that moved" is measured literally — any of your own discs whose resting position differs from where the shot found it.

    Physics, briefly

    • Fixed timestep: 4 substeps of 1/960 s per 1/240 s tick, accumulated against the frame clock. At the maximum shot speed a disc advances about 0.117″ per substep, against a 0.81″ disc-plus-peg contact distance and a 1″ chamfer, so nothing tunnels and no lip crossing is skipped.
    • Sliding friction is a constant deceleration (about 78 in/s², multiplied on the Crown) with a stop threshold, so a shot always terminates. The lip refund is exactly the lip toll, so a round trip over the Crown is energy-neutral and cannot pump.
    • Substep order is fixed: capture the pre-integration position and Crown flag, apply friction, integrate, relax contacts, resolve rails, then test the lip crossing against the captured flag, then remove anything past a ditch. The lip is tested after de-overlap so a disc shoved across the boundary by a positional correction cannot mount for free.
    • Disc–disc: reduced mass — impulse J = −(1+e)·vn / (1/ma + 1/mb) along the contact normal, with the de-overlap split by inverse mass so the heavier disc gives less ground. Restitution is the pair's minimum. Disc–peg: immovable circle, the disc's own pegE. Rail: position clamp plus the disc's own railE and a layout-wide tangential drag, so a cushion strictly removes energy and can never tunnel.
    • Because everything above is per disc, the machine's search sees every stat and every in-sim effect for free — it is running the same function. The one stat it cannot see that way is the hand's sigma, which is what the stage-two pass is for.
    • The machine's search runs this identical solver headlessly. Same seed and same released angle/power give bit-identical resting positions.

    The opponent

    Each turn it builds candidate (placement, angle, power) triples from five points along its launch line — straight at every live opponent disc, at the Crown across the climb band, at each of the Crown's own faces, through every gate the picket declares, off each rail at the mirror image of the Crown and of every target, a two-cushion line to the Crown, and a coarse grid — simulates every one to rest, and scores the resulting board:

    The gate and face candidates are there because of the boards above: on the Courtyard and the Gauntlet the straight line at the Crown is a shot into a post, so a search that only generated straight lines and rail mirrors would be searching routes the board exists to forbid. They are still only candidates — every one is simulated with the same solver you are watching, so nothing here is an assertion about what will happen.

      It picks the best-scoring candidate, then adds Gaussian noise to the released angle and power. Because the rail route is a named route on this board, the bank candidates matter: without them the machine would be a liar by omission on exactly the shot the rectangle exists to teach.
