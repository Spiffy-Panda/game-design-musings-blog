The Regulation Board

A regulation crokinole board, drawn from the real numbers and simulated with a fixed-timestep rigid-disc solver. The opponent is the point: it searches shots by running the same physics you're watching, scores the resulting board with terms you can read, and then lets a shaky hand ruin the plan.

Mouse / touch: drag back from your disc and release — slingshot. Click elsewhere on your arc to slide the disc along the line.
Keyboard (click the board first): ←→ aim · ↑↓ power · AD slide along the line · Space shoot · hold Shift for fine steps.

    Rules enforced

    • Placement. Your disc is set flat inside your own quadrant, touching the starting line. You choose where along that arc; the simulator keeps the disc centre on the line and clamps the angle to your quadrant.
    • The contact rule. If the opponent has any disc on the surface, your shot must touch one — directly or through a carom off your own disc. A shot that touches none is a foul: your shooting disc is removed, and so is every one of your own discs that moved during the shot. The opponent's discs stay where the shot left them.
    • Empty board. When the opponent has no discs down, your shot must finish in the guarded 15-ring (or in the hole). Anything else is removed under the same penalty. This is why the opening shot of a round is an open 20 attempt and not a quiet park.
    • The ditch. No walls. A disc whose centre passes the edge of the playing surface is gone for the round. A disc that comes to rest outside the starting line is also removed — the 2″ band outside that line is a launch pad, not a scoring zone.
    • The 20-hole. A disc that settles in the hole is lifted out and banked as 20 for its owner, whoever shot it.
    • Scoring a round. Add up ring values plus 20 per banked disc for each side. Only the higher side scores, and it scores the difference. Then the lead alternates. First to 100.

    Where this board approximates

    Ring value is decided by the disc's centre. The tournament rule is stricter and line-based: a disc touching a line takes the lower value, so it must be wholly inside a ring to earn it. Centre-based scoring is a small, uniform simplification and it is applied consistently — including to the "outside the starting line is dead" rule and the empty-board 15-ring rule.

    The hole swallows a little more than it should. Geometrically, a 1¼″ disc is wholly inside a 1 3/8″ hole only when its centre is within 1/16″ of dead centre. Real holes have a lip that tips a leaning disc in, so a disc here drops when it comes to rest with its centre within 5/16″. It is a sink-on-rest test, not a sink-on-pass one: a disc travelling fast over the hole keeps going.

    No spin, no bevel, no thickness. Discs are flat circles of equal mass. Real crokinole discs are dished and can ride up on each other; that is not modelled.

    Foul bookkeeping. "Discs that moved" is measured literally — any of your own discs whose resting position differs from where the shot found it.

    Physics, briefly

    • Fixed timestep: 4 substeps of 1/960 s per 1/240 s tick, accumulated against the frame clock. At the maximum shot speed a disc advances about 0.11″ per substep against a 0.81″ disc-plus-peg contact distance, so nothing tunnels.
    • Sliding friction is a constant deceleration (about 78 in/s²) with a stop threshold, so a shot always terminates.
    • Disc–disc: equal mass, restitution 0.95, impulse along the contact normal, plus positional de-overlap. Disc–peg: immovable circle, restitution 0.6 (tournament pegs are rubber-covered and eat energy).
    • The machine's search runs this identical solver headlessly. Same seed and same released angle/power give bit-identical resting positions.

    The opponent

    Each turn it builds candidate (placement, angle, power) triples — shots aimed at every opponent disc, shots aimed at the hole, a coarse grid, and then a refinement pass around the best of those — simulates every one to rest, and scores the resulting board:

      It picks the best-scoring candidate, then adds Gaussian noise to the released angle and power. The difficulty slider is that noise. A perfect search with a 4° hand is a worse opponent than a narrow search with a still one — which is the honest way round, because that is also true of people.

      One file, no build step, no network. Physics, rules, opponent and rendering are separate sections of a single inline script.
