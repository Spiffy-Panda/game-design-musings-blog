Crokinole

The board is the ruleset.

A dexterity game from 1870s Ontario, taken apart by rebuilding it. Simulating a physical game is the fastest way to find out how much of its design was never written down — because a simulator cannot leave anything implicit.

The bet

Crokinole is a round board, a hole in the middle, eight pegs, and two people taking turns flicking small wooden discs at each other. No cards, no dice, no hidden information, no text anywhere on the equipment. Writing a design note about it ought to be like writing a design note about a frisbee.

The bet is that this is precisely what makes it interesting. Most games keep their rules in a book and their state in components; crokinole keeps both in one piece of plywood. The rules are inscribed as geometry — and once you notice that, the object stops being a board and starts being a compiled ruleset, one that has been iterated on by furniture makers for a century and a half without anyone editing a single sentence.

The fastest way to decompile it is to rebuild it. The Board is a playable crokinole: regulation dimensions in inches, a fixed-timestep rigid-disc solver, the contact rule enforced, and an opponent that searches by running the same physics you are watching. Nothing about it is clever. Its value is that every hand-wave had to become a number, and the hand-waves turned out to be where the design lives.

What the rebuild made explicit

Three things stopped being scenery the moment they had to compile.

The dimensions are load-bearing. A 26″ playing surface; a 24″ starting line you must shoot from; a 16″ circle worth 10 and an 8″ circle worth 15; a 1 3/8″ hole worth 20 and 1¼″ discs to put in it. Those are quoted numbers. The one that isn't quoted is the peg phase. Eight 3/8″ pegs sit with their centres on the 8″ circle, 45° apart — but at what rotation? The simulator uses 22.5° off each quadrant line, which puts two pegs per quadrant straddling its centre line, and that is derived from the geometry, not cited from a source: it is the only phase under which both the quadrant divider and the middle of your own quadrant look straight down a gap. Get it wrong and there is no open twenty at all. A rotation nobody writes down is the difference between crokinole and a shrug.

The contact rule is doing all the balancing. On paper it is a one-line restriction: if your opponent has a disc on the surface, your shot must touch one. In code it is a foul check that removes your shooting disc and every disc of yours that moved, while leaving theirs wherever the shot pushed them. That asymmetry is the whole tempo of the game, and it only became obvious once it was written as an if.

Difficulty had nowhere to hide. A physical crokinole player's variance is their own hand; a digital one has to be manufactured. The simulator's difficulty control is a Gaussian tremor applied to the released angle and power — 1.2° by default, up to 4° — and the search is identical at every setting. Turning the dial does not make the machine smarter, only steadier. That is honest, and it is also flat: the opponent has one personality at every level. It is the live tension in the design, and it is stated on the page rather than hidden behind a "Hard" label.

Six claims

  1. CRK.1The board is the ruleset.

    Nearly every rule in crokinole is a geometric fact already sitting on the plywood: the rings are the scoring table, the ditch is the out-of-bounds clause, the hole is the jackpot. The rulebook is mostly a description of an object. Very few games can be read off their own equipment.

  2. CRK.2The contact rule taxes the lead.

    Your discs on the board are simultaneously your score and your opponent's permission to shoot freely: if they have a target they must hit it, and if they miss they lose the shot and everything they moved. Scoring well hands the opponent an obligation and something to knock at. The catch-up mechanism is not a rule bolted on top — it is the same discs, counted twice.

  3. CRK.3The hand is the randomiser.

    No dice, no shuffle, no hidden information — the variance is entirely your own motor control. Digitising the game therefore means choosing a noise model, and that choice is the whole of the difficulty design. Here it is Gaussian tremor on release angle and power: the machine never gets cleverer, only steadier. Honest, and flat, and the most interesting unsolved part.

  4. CRK.4Eight pegs turn geometry into repertoire.

    An open plane has infinite shots and no vocabulary. Eight posts guarding the 15-ring cut it into named lanes — the open twenty straight down your quadrant's centre line, the ricochet off the near post, the hide tucked behind a peg. Obstacles are what make a skill learnable: they give it landmarks to be good at.

  5. CRK.5Only the leader scores, and only the margin.

    Round scoring is differential — the higher side banks the difference — so a round is a tug-of-war rather than an accumulation, and a blowout on the board converts to a modest number. The scoreboard actively refuses to compound. Twelve discs of work can be worth five points.

  6. CRK.6A legible opponent beats a strong one.

    The machine searches on the order of 160 candidate shots a turn with the same deterministic physics the player gets, then says which scoring term actually won — measured as the improvement over leaving the board alone, because raw board value made "my discs are worth points" win every single time and explained nothing. An opponent that narrates its reason is a teacher; one that merely wins is weather.

The pages

I · The simulator

The Board — play a round →

A regulation board at 26″, twelve discs a side shot alternately, the contact rule and the empty-board 15-ring rule enforced, the ditch fatal. Drag back from your disc and release like a slingshot, or aim with the keyboard. The physics is deterministic and seeded — same seed, same release, same resting positions — and the opponent shows its search: the shot it chose, how far its hand drifted, and the scoring terms that decided it.

The Curves

Drive the deterministic solver headlessly and plot what the hand model actually does: open-twenty hit rate against tremor σ, takeout success by ring, foul rate as the board fills. The difficulty dial stops being a feeling and becomes a graph.

Where it bends the real game

Two departures worth naming up front. Ring value is decided by the disc's centre, where the tournament rule is line-based — a disc touching a line takes the lower value — so this board is very slightly generous at every boundary, uniformly. And singles here is twelve discs a side: the general rule sets say twelve, while World Crokinole Championship singles plays eight. The simulator's own rules panel lists the rest of the approximations, including how much the hole is allowed to swallow.

Everything runs in the page — one file, no build step, no network, no telemetry; nothing you do leaves the tab. Handles CRK.1–CRK.6 above are stable and append-only: new claims take new numbers, nothing renumbers, so they can be cited from other pages.
